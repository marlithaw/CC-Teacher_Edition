The Standard

Listening Is A Transfer

Listening
Information actually moved from them to you. You can prove it by restating it.
Waiting
You were drafting your reply. Nothing transferred. It only looked like a conversation.
Why this worksGroups that distribute airtime make measurably better decisions than groups where a few people dominate — not because it is fairer, but because more information reaches the table.

Slide 1 · Title & Promise R2

“In every group there are people who take up most of the air and people who never get any. Neither is a character flaw. But if we never name it, the same four people talk for a year and everyone else checks out.”

Look for

  • Students self-locate honestly
  • Quiet students recognize themselves — watch for relief, not shame

If it breaks

  • If it becomes about blaming talkers: “This is not a callout. Talkers are not villains. We are looking at distribution.”

Support adult

  • Track actual airtime during the session and share the numbers

Language support

  • Pre-teach 'airtime' as an idiom.
  • Multilingual students are often over-represented in the quiet group for language reasons, not preference — name that.

Visual: Group icon. Consider a simple bar chart of airtime distribution as a discussion prompt.

Slide 2 · Regulate R1

“Four in, four hold, four out. The long exhale slows your heart rate through the vagus nerve. You do not have to believe in it. Three rounds.”

Look for

  • Genuine rather than performative participation
  • Resisters who join by day three — that is the win

If it breaks

  • If a student refuses: “Sit quietly and let us work.” Refusal here is usually self-consciousness, not defiance.

Support adult

  • Participate visibly. Adolescents read adult non-participation instantly.

Language support

  • Numbers carry it.
  • Pre-teach 'vagus nerve' or say 'the nerve that controls heart rate.'

Visual: Lungs, clock, calm icons; optional heart-rate line dropping.

Slide 3 · Orient R2, R3

“Listening is a transfer of information, not a display of politeness. If nothing moved from their head to yours, you were not listening — you were queuing. And research on group decision-making is blunt about this: groups where a few people dominate make worse decisions.”

Look for

  • Students engage with the mechanism rather than the manners framing
  • Students connect distribution to decision quality

If it breaks

  • If dismissed as soft: “This is not about being nice. It is about whether your group gets the information it needs.”

Support adult

  • Chart counterarguments seriously

Language support

  • Pre-teach 'transfer' and 'distribute' in this context.

Visual: Opposed ear/speech icons. Optional: two arrows, one completing the transfer, one bouncing back.

Slide 4 · Connect R3, R4

“New rule and it is a hard one: you do not get to disagree with someone until you can state their position well enough that they say yes, that is what I meant. That is not a courtesy. It is how you make sure you are arguing with what they actually said.”

Look for

  • Restatements the partner confirms as accurate
  • Students catching themselves arguing with a version their partner did not say

If it breaks

  • “You just argued with something they did not say. Restate first.”

Support adult

  • Model this with a student volunteer before releasing to pairs

Language support

  • This protocol transfers directly to academic argumentation.
  • Provide: ‘So your position is ___. Is that right?’

Visual: Two-people icon. Consider a 2-minute countdown.

Slide 5 · Practice R3, R4, R5

“Pick the row that is actually you. Frequent talkers — wait for two other voices. Quiet folks — one contribution, that is the whole ask. And everyone invites one person in by name. Naming someone is the single most effective move here.”

Look for

  • Frequent contributors visibly holding back
  • Quiet students contributing once — count it as full success

If it breaks

  • If the same voices dominate: “Two new voices before anyone repeats.” Enforce it neutrally.

Support adult

  • Track who has spoken and invite by name yourself

Language support

  • 'One contribution is enough' lowers the affective filter for developing English speakers.

Visual: Three role-specific icons. Students self-select which row applies to them.

Slide 6 · Commit R2, R5

“Specific person, specific moment. 'I will listen better' is not a commitment, it is a mood. 'I will restate what Marcus says in fourth period before I respond' — that I can see.”

Look for

  • Named person or moment
  • Observable verb

If it breaks

  • “Give me the video, not the caption.”

Support adult

  • Record and reconnect in the afternoon block

Language support

  • Offer a verb bank: restate, invite, wait, ask.

Visual: Check icon.

Slide 7 · Transition R1, R4, R5

“How we leave is how we enter. A clean exit means the next class starts on time instead of burning ninety seconds. That is time you get back.”

Look for

  • Chairs in unprompted
  • Collective timing, no racing

If it breaks

  • “Reset. First thirty seconds again, clean.”

Support adult

  • Position yourself at the destination

Language support

  • Icons carry the sequence.
  • Post the time.

Visual: Four step icons plus a clock with the class's previous time.
