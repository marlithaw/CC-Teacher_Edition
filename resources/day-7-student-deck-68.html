Why Apologies Fail

Intent Is Not The Repair

“I didn't mean to”
About your intent. They are dealing with impact.
“I'm sorry you felt”
Moves the problem into them. Not a repair.
“I did X. Next time Y.”
Names the action and the change.
Why this worksRepair changes future behavior. An apology that changes nothing gives no reason to trust you differently.

The Repair Move

Absorb

Name the change

Slide 1 · Title & Promise R2, R6

“Somebody has apologized to you and then done the exact same thing a week later. You remember how that felt — worse than no apology, because now you know they knew. Today is about not being that person.”

Look for

  • Recognition — this lands hard at this age
  • Students distinguishing performance from repair

If it breaks

  • Name a real repair you owed someone and what you changed.

Support adult

  • Adolescents are acutely sensitive to adult hypocrisy here. Only teach this if you are willing to model it.

Language support

  • Pre-teach 'repair' as distinct from 'apology.'

Visual: Repair icon. Open with a specific mistake you made and what you actually changed.

Slide 2 · Regulate R1

“Four in, four hold, four out. The long exhale slows your heart rate through the vagus nerve. You do not have to believe in it. Three rounds.”

Look for

  • Genuine rather than performative participation
  • Resisters who join by day three — that is the win

If it breaks

  • If a student refuses: “Sit quietly and let us work.” Refusal here is usually self-consciousness, not defiance.

Support adult

  • Participate visibly. Adolescents read adult non-participation instantly.

Language support

  • Numbers carry it.
  • Pre-teach 'vagus nerve' or say 'the nerve that controls heart rate.'

Visual: Lungs, clock, calm icons; optional heart-rate line dropping.

Slide 3 · Orient R6

“'I didn't mean to' is about you. 'I'm sorry you felt that way' moves the problem into them — that one is genuinely worse than silence. The only version that works names what you did and what changes.”

Look for

  • Students identifying which failed form they default to
  • Students catching these forms in others, including adults

If it breaks

  • If a student defends 'I didn't mean to': “Did it change what happened to them?”

Support adult

  • Expect students to name adults who use these forms. Do not defend colleagues reflexively.

Language support

  • Pre-teach the three forms as fixed phrases.

Visual: Three icons showing two failed forms and one working form.

Slide 4 · Connect R6

“An apology you got that changed nothing. What would have actually repaired it? Be specific — not 'meant it,' but what would they have had to DO?”

Look for

  • Concrete alternative repairs named
  • Students moving from 'be sincere' to 'do something different'

If it breaks

  • If answers stay at sincerity: “Sincerity is invisible. What action would you have seen?”

Support adult

  • Some examples will involve staff. Receive them.

Language support

  • Allow the initial account in a home language.

Visual: Two-people icon.

Slide 5 · Practice R6

“Name the action. Then absorb — they respond and you say nothing. No context, no 'but,' no explanation. That step is where almost everyone fails, and it is the step that proves you meant it. Then name a change specific enough that they could check.”

Look for

  • Silence held during absorb
  • Changes specific enough to be verified

If it breaks

  • “You explained. Back to absorb.”

Support adult

  • Practice on low-stakes scenarios. Never stage a real conflict in front of the group.

Language support

  • All three sentences are provided as models.

Visual: Three repair-step icons with the middle one emphasized.

Slide 6 · Commit R2, R5, R6

“Most of you are carrying one. A person you owe a repair to. Name them privately — this one does not get shared out. Then name the action, and do it this week.”

Look for

  • Genuine engagement rather than deflection
  • Students naming actions rather than intentions

If it breaks

  • Do not push a student who will not engage. Some repairs are not school business.

Support adult

  • Keep these private. Do not share out. Follow up individually with any student who seems to be carrying something heavy.

Language support

  • Allow the private note in a home language.

Visual: Check icon. This may be the most consequential commitment of the ten days.

Slide 7 · Transition R1, R4, R5

“How we leave is how we enter. A clean exit means the next class starts on time instead of burning ninety seconds. That is time you get back.”

Look for

  • Chairs in unprompted
  • Collective timing, no racing

If it breaks

  • “Reset. First thirty seconds again, clean.”

Support adult

  • Position yourself at the destination

Language support

  • Icons carry the sequence.
  • Post the time.

Visual: Four step icons plus a clock with the class's previous time.
